# affects

AFFECTS

Affect theory studies how emotions, sensations, and energies circulate among bodies and collectives, shaping experience, politics, and knowledge. Affects are not only individual feelings but relational forces that create bonds, drive movements, and sustain resistances.
Affects are the sensible memory transmitted through songs, dances, rituals, and care. They are the shared pain that becomes political force, the joy that arises as an insurgent practice against dispossession. But they are also spaces of dispute—political territories where ways of inhabiting the world are reinvented.
The term affect has roots in modern philosophy, especially in Baruch Spinoza (Ethics, 1677), who defined it as variations in a body’s capacity to exist in relation with others. For him, affects increase or diminish the capacity to act, and they are always relational.
In the 20th century, phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty) and poststructuralism (Deleuze and Guattari) revisited this notion. Affect theory as a field emerged in the 1990s, propelled by cultural, queer, and feminist studies. Brian Massumi (1995), in his reading of A Thousand Plateaus by Deleuze and Guattari, defined affect as pre-conscious intensity that circulates among bodies before being coded as emotion or feeling.
Later, Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick (2003) and Sara Ahmed (2004) linked affects with queer and feminist studies, showing how shame, pride, fear, or happiness are socially and politically constructed.
Thus, affect theory consolidated as an interdisciplinary field in philosophy, anthropology, sociology, and cultural studies. Unlike traditional psychology, affects are not understood only as individual experiences but as collective forces that configure social, cultural, and political life: fear in contexts of war, collective pain in processes of historical memory, joy as political force in social movements.
Contributions from the Global South :
Frantz Fanon (1952) showed how racialization produces affective alienation in colonized bodies.
María Lugones (2008) analyzed how the coloniality of gender regulates and controls affects and subjectivities.
Achille Mbembe (2003) linked the control of life and death with collective affects such as fear and violence.
Catherine Walsh and Walter Mignolo highlighted how affects are transmitted in the memories and community practices—rituals, songs, dances, care—of Indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples.
Today, affect theory is applied to the analysis of populism, racism, migration, feminist, queer, and decolonial struggles—understanding that affects not only reflect the social but produce it: they configure solidarities, exclusions, resistances, and ways of inhabiting the world.
